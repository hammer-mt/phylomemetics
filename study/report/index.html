Phylomemetics · Batman 1943–2022

Neurons inside AI models can define memes. They find Batman's core canon and trace films back to their source comics. Plain word counts trace the sources about as well

Memetics, the attempt to study culture the way genetics studies genes, stalled because nobody could say where one meme ends and the next begins. We defined a meme as whatever makes a single neuron inside an AI model fire, traced the memes that make up Batman across 80 years, checked them against a second model built by a different company, and ran three pre-registered experiments (each prediction written down before it ran) on identity, heredity and selection. After the first analysis, a second review re-ran the work and tested every claim against tougher checks, including plain word counts that use no AI model. Some claims came out narrower, and this page gives the revised results.

Background

New to memes? Start here

  1. A meme is a piece of culture that gets copied.

    The biologist Richard Dawkins coined the word in The Selfish Gene (1976) for "a unit of cultural transmission, or a unit of imitation." His examples were tunes, ideas, catch-phrases, clothes fashions and ways of making pots or building arches, which spread from brain to brain by imitation, as genes spread from parent to child. This is the original, broader sense of the word, older than internet image memes. In Batman, memes are things like Alfred the butler, the secret identity, the origin wound (a hero made by a childhood loss) and Two-Face's coin.

  2. Memetics hoped to make this a science, and stalled.

    Memetics set out to study memes the way genetics studies genes: how they are copied, how they change and which ones win. It stalled because nobody could say where one meme ends and the next begins, so nobody could count them.

  3. An AI language model is built from neurons.

    The models behind chatbots contain hundreds of thousands of small units called neurons. Many work like a smoke detector with one trigger: quiet on most text, they fire when they meet one particular thing, such as a funeral or a butler.

  4. The idea here: a meme is whatever makes one neuron fire.

    That gives every meme an address inside the model. The secret identity, for example, is L13·8608+: layer 13, neuron 8,608, positive end. Memes can then be counted, named from the sentences that set them off, and followed across 80 years of Batman stories.

  5. What the models read.

    The models read Wikipedia plot summaries: 32 Batman films, comics and games, 13 related stories such as Zorro and Spider-Man, and 69 ordinary films with no superheroes as a baseline. A neuron counts as one of Batman's memes when it fires more strongly on a Batman summary than on almost every ordinary film, in at least 6 of the 32 Batman works. Claude, an AI model, names the memes from the sentences they fire on most. The main model is Qwen3-8B, an open model from Alibaba (8B means 8 billion learned numbers). A second model trained separately on different data, Meta's Llama 3.1 8B, checks the results.

  6. Stories can have family trees.

    Biologists build family trees of species from the genes they share, and researchers have done the same for folktales such as Little Red Riding Hood, using plot details coded by hand. Here the shared traits are neuron-memes, so we can ask which Batman stories are close relatives and whether a film inherits memes from the comic it adapts. Family trees (phylogenies) of memes are what the project's name, phylomemetics, refers to.

  7. The real test: do neurons see more than words?

    If two stories share words like "butler" and "cave", simply counting shared words already links them, with no AI needed. Neurons are worth the trouble only if they catch shared ideas told in different words, so the main results are checked against plain word counts.

Glossary: the terms used on this page
Address
Where a neuron sits in a model. L13·8608+ means layer 13, neuron 8,608, positive end; each neuron has a positive (+) and a negative (−) end, counted separately.
AUC
A score for how well a measure tells two groups apart: the chance that a story picked at random from one group scores higher than one from the other, where 0.5 is a coin toss and 1 means always.
Billionaire Vigilante Orphans
A proposed family of heroes that puts Batman with the Scarlet Pimpernel, Zorro and Iron Man, and on this page Puss in Boots too. Phase 1 checks which of Batman's memes each one carries.
Canon
The memes in nearly every Batman work. The page grades memes by how many of the 32 works carry them: must have (at least 29, the core canon), usually there (23 to 28) and optional (6 to 22). A meme is Batman's own if no more than 3 of the 13 relatives carry it. Phase 2 uses its own versions: E1's canon is made of the single neurons present in at least 80% of the other 31 Batman works, and the heredity test uses a band of less common memes, in 2 to 16 works.
Centroid
The average word profile of the Batman stories; a story scores higher the closer its own words come to that average.
Cohen's kappa
Agreement between two present-or-absent grids after removing the agreement expected by chance: 1 means identical, 0 means no better than chance.
Confidence interval
The range a score would plausibly take with a different sample of stories; a 95% interval (CI), or bootstrap interval, is drawn as a line on the charts, and a wide one means few examples.
Corpus
The collection of texts studied: Wikipedia plot summaries of 32 Batman works, 13 relatives and 69 ordinary films.
Correlation (r, ρ)
A number from −1 to 1 for how closely two sets of numbers rise and fall together: 1 means in step, 0 means unrelated, −1 means opposite; ρ (rho) does the same with rankings.
Embeddings
Lists of numbers an AI model produces to sum up a whole text, so that texts with similar meanings get similar numbers.
Fire, present
A neuron fires on a sentence when its signal is unusually strong there. It counts as present in a work when its average signal on the work's three strongest sentences is higher than its score on 68 of the 69 ordinary films.
Heredity
Whether a work inherits memes from the work it was adapted from, the way a child inherits genes from a parent.
Hub adjustment
A correction for comics that resemble every film, so that a comic can't look like a film's source just by being typical.
Identity
Whether some set of memes marks a story as Batman, even with the names taken out.
Llama 3.1 8B
The second AI model, from Meta, used as an independent check. "8B" means 8 billion parameters, the numbers a model learns in training.
Meme
Dawkins's word for a unit of culture copied from person to person, such as a tune or a catch-phrase; on this page, whatever makes one neuron fire.
Meme family
Neurons that fire on the same sentences, merged and counted as one meme: in Qwen3-8B, 18,485 candidate neurons merge into 14,843 memes.
Memetics
The attempt to study how memes are copied, change and spread, the way genetics studies genes.
Multiverse
Rerunning one analysis under every combination of reasonable settings, to check that the result does not hang on one choice. E2 was run 30,240 ways.
Name-free text
A plot summary with every name replaced by a neutral code ("X1 interrogates X2"), so a neuron can't spot Batman from the word "Batman" or "Gotham".
Neuron
One small unit inside an AI language model that gives a strong signal for some text and stays quiet for the rest. This study counts each neuron's two ends (strongly positive and strongly negative) separately, which gives 884,736 in Qwen3-8B.
Null (chance baseline)
The spread of results that chance alone would give, made by shuffling labels many times, for example by pretending a random comic is a film's source. This page calls it the chance baseline. A null result means no effect was found.
p-value
How often chance alone would give a result at least this strong; smaller is stronger, and below 0.05 is the usual bar. An exact p is worked out over every possible relabelling, not a random sample of them.
Phylomemetics
Building family trees of memes, the way phylogenetics builds family trees of species from shared genes.
Placebo film
A Batman film, animated film or game that adapts no comic, given the real adaptations' sources anyway. It should show no inheritance, so it checks that the method doesn't find heredity where there is none.
Power
The chance that a test would detect an effect if there really were one.
Pre-registration
Writing down a prediction, and the result that would count as failure, before running the experiment, so the goalposts can't move afterwards.
Qwen3-8B
The main AI model, an openly available model from Alibaba with 8 billion parameters. Qwen3-1.7B is a smaller model from the same family.
Relative (cousin)
One of the 13 non-Batman stories used for comparison: five older books and tales (Puss in Boots, The Count of Monte Cristo, Sherlock Holmes in A Study in Scarlet, Dracula, The Scarlet Pimpernel) and eight masked-hero and superhero films, from The Bat (1926) and Zorro to Spider-Man and Iron Man.
Selection
Whether some memes spread further than others because of something about them or the works that carry them. Here: whether memes from popular early works spread further.
Sequel check
A sanity check for the heredity test, also called the positive control: without names, each sequel should place its prequel among its two closest earlier screen works, for at least 5 of 7 sequels.
Steering
Turning a neuron up or down and checking that the model's writing changes the way its label predicts. It is a stronger test of a label than reading the sentences the neuron fires on.
Twin
For a Qwen meme, the neuron in a second model (Llama 3.1 8B or Qwen3-1.7B) whose firing across the same 3,700 sentences matches it best. The correlation r says how close the match is.
Word counts (TF-IDF)
Ways to compare texts with no AI model. Word presence notes which words a text contains; TF-IDF counts them and gives extra weight to words that are rare across the corpus.
Word-like neuron
A neuron whose firing a single word predicts well, meme or not. Such neurons are a fair comparison for twins, because two models tend to share neurons for the same word.
Phase 2

Three experiments: identity, heredity, selection

With a way to measure memes, can we test the three things a science of memes needs? They follow the logic of genetics: what the unit is, whether it is passed on, and whether some versions win out. Here they are identity (is there a set of memes that makes a story Batman?), heredity (do works inherit memes from the works they adapt?) and selection (do memes spread further when they ride on a hit?). Each experiment was written down, with the result that would count as failure, and attacked by three independent AI reviewers before it was run.

Unfamiliar term? See the glossary above the tabs.

Heredity is the clearest result. Films and games sit closer to their documented source comic than to other comics, even with every name removed, in both models (exact p = 0.0026 and 0.00017; a p-value is how often chance alone would give a result this strong, so smaller is stronger). Rerun under 30,240 combinations of reasonable settings, the pattern holds in 99.6% of them. But plain word overlap on the same texts finds the sources as well. The test counts how many other comics look more like the film than its real source does: 11% on TF-IDF, a standard word-count method that uses no AI model, against 16% on the neurons of Qwen (the main model) and 10% on Llama's (the second). Lower is closer, and chance gives 47%. The pre-registered test of whether the neurons add anything beyond shared words fails for Qwen, the primary model, and passes for Llama. So the films carry their source's plot content, and neurons detect it about as well as word counts do.

The identity test failed as stated. Without names, Batman's canon (the memes in nearly every Batman story) scores Batman stories no higher than other superhero films. That failure comes from the way the canon was scored. A word-count centroid, the average word profile of a Batman story, still separates Batman from the superhero films on the same name-free texts (AUC 0.80, where 0.5 is a coin toss and 1 is a perfect split), mostly on crime-city words. The selection test found nothing. With 15 early works it had little power, meaning little chance of spotting a real effect, so finding nothing says little about whether selection happens.

Not detected

The memes of popular early works did not spread further than those of obscure ones (rank correlation ρ = 0.25 and 0.00, p = 0.35 and 0.58). The test had about a 27% chance, roughly 1 in 4, of detecting a moderate real link (a correlation of 0.3).

The name-free core: the superhero template

The core is close to the textbook definition of a superhero, which the comics scholar Peter Coogan gives as a mission, powers and an identity: here a self-imposed mission, a costume and a secret identity. E2 · Do films inherit their source comic's memes?

Question
Nine films and games adapt a comic in our set. Do they carry more of their source's optional memes than other comics', with names removed? Here optional means less common, in 2 to 16 of the 32 Batman works, because memes found in nearly every story can't show which comic a film came from.
Prediction
Fewer comics beat the documented source, by looking more like the film, than chance would give: one-sided p < 0.05, where one-sided means only a gap in the predicted direction counts. Checked against comics that resemble everything, comics published after the film, and placebo films (Batman films and games that adapt no comic, given the same sources). A second, separate check, whose failure would count for less because inheritance would still be shown: if the effect disappears once shared words are removed, the neurons add nothing that word counts don't already catch.
Result
16% (Qwen) and 10% (Llama) of comics beat the source, against 47% under the planned chance baseline; exact p = 0.0026 and 0.00017. Word overlap alone gives 11%. With shared words removed, Qwen's effect is no longer significant and Llama's is.

In both models the real source comic sits at or near the front for most films

Each row is an adaptation; each grey slot is one comic it could have drawn from (only comics published no later than the film). The dot shows where the documented source ranks by inherited memes, name-free.

Names carried less of this than expected. With names left in, the share of comics beating the source falls only from 16% to 12% (Qwen) and from 10% to 8% (Llama). Most of the inheritance is in what happens to the characters.

The pattern itself is very robust. An independent rebuild from the raw activations (the neurons' recorded signals) gives the same numbers. Rerun under 30,240 combinations of reasonable settings, p is below 0.05 in 99.6% of them; the details are under "Every robustness check" below. Dropping any one, two or three adaptations, or any one comic, never breaks it under the planned chance baseline. It is less secure under a stricter baseline that only swaps comics within an era: there some of Qwen's leave-outs do fail (37 of 84 triples, the ways of leaving out three adaptations). Llama's never do.

Lower means closer to the documented source. Under each label: the exact p-value, and the sequel check. What the films inherit is plot content, and word counts see it too. TF-IDF puts 11% of comics ahead of the source and word presence 17%, against 16% and 10% for the neurons. Both word measures also pass the sequel check (6 of 7), a test that the method can match a sequel to its prequel, which Qwen's neurons miss (4 of 7).

The pre-registration also set a separate test, the only one that can show the neurons add something beyond shared words: the neurons should still find the source after removing what word overlap explains, by subtracting the part of the neurons' scores that shared words predict. This was planned but not run at first; it has been run now. For Qwen, the primary model, the neurons fail it (p = 0.25 after removing TF-IDF, 0.11 after word presence, 0.09 after both). For Llama they pass (p = 0.033, 0.014 and 0.010). TF-IDF keeps its signal with the neurons removed (p < 0.001 in both models). So Llama's neurons add a little beyond words, and Qwen's add nothing we can detect.

The planned chance figure was too high, which made the result look stronger than it is. The 47% comes from relabelling the comics freely: pretending, over and over, that other comics are the sources, and seeing how well those fake sources score. Fairer versions give lower figures. Relabelling only with comics that existed at the time gives about 42%, and relabelling only within an era about 39%. The result holds against each (Qwen p = 0.011 and 0.026; Llama p = 0.0006 and 0.0014). But without the three Killing Joke adaptations (Batman '89, The Dark Knight and Joker), Qwen's within-era p rises to 0.083, above the usual 0.05 bar.

The placebo check had a bug. Placebo films are Batman films and games that adapt no comic, given the real adaptations' sources; they should score near chance. Two of the 11 placebo films, the 1943 serial and Batman '66, came out before every comic in the set, so they had no eligible comic and were scored a perfect 0. That pulled the placebo average down to 38% (Qwen) and 39% (Llama). The 9 valid placebo films average 46% (Qwen) and 47% (Llama), and the real adaptations sit below all 9. So the check passes with the 9 valid films. Run as the plan wrote it, with all 11, it fails, so using 9 is a departure from the plan.

Every robustness check

The 30,240 versions vary the presence bar (how strongly a neuron must fire to count), the frequency band (which memes count as optional), the source rule (how to score a film with more than one source), the hub adjustment (a correction for comics that resemble every film), the time rule (only comics published no later than the film count) and more. Every version that fails has the hub adjustment switched off, so that correction matters.

Sequel check: do sequels find their prequel without names?

If the method has power without names, each sequel should rank its prequel among the top 2 earlier screen works. The bar was 5 of 7: Qwen reached 4, Llama 5 (with names, 7 and 6). TF-IDF and word presence both reach 6.

E3 · Do memes ride on hits?

Question
Do the memes of popular early works (before 2000) turn up more in later works than the memes of obscure ones?
Prediction
Positive rank correlation (more popular works ranking higher on spread) between an early work's popularity (Wikipedia views per month) and how far its memes spread, with adaptations removed so fame from being adapted can't drive it.
Result
ρ = 0.25 (Qwen, p = 0.35) and 0.00 (Llama, p = 0.58). On the original texts, with names, both are negative (−0.32 and −0.50), the opposite direction to the prediction.

No link between an early work's popularity and how far its memes spread

Each dot is one of the 15 Batman works before 2000 (Qwen3-8B, name-free). Up means its memes reappear in later works more than their earlier frequency predicts, after accounting for the work's year, length and how typical it is.

With 15 works, only a strong effect could have shown up. A simulation of the same permutation test (which compares the result with many random shufflings of the popularity figures) on 15 works gives it a 27% chance of detecting a true moderate correlation of 0.3, and 54% for a strong one of 0.5. So this result is inconclusive, and it says little either way about whether popularity matters. A planned extra check, which used the ordinary films as the outcome, turned out to give every meme nearly the same score whatever the data, so it has been dropped. Pageviews are also measured today, after decades of adaptation. A real test of selection needs a bigger lineage and success measured at release.

How we tried not to fool ourselves

  • Written down first. Each experiment's prediction and the result that would count as failure were written down before it was run. The saved files' timestamps cannot confirm that order on their own. The session transcript in the repository records the reviews being read and the experiment code revised before the run began.
  • Attacked before running. Three AI reviewers were asked to break each design. They found real problems, and most of the safeguards below came from them.
  • Truly name-free texts. A rewriter first replaced names with role words, but called Batman "the vigilante" more than 300 times and Spider-Man "the hero". That leaks identity, so we used the rewrite only to find the names, then replaced each with a neutral code.
  • No marking our own homework. The films that set the presence bar are never scored against it. The ordinary films are split in half 20 times, and one half sets the bar for the other.
  • Breaking circular loops. E3 drops every adaptation from the later works, because a comic's fame partly comes from being adapted. E2 counts only comics published no later than the film.
  • Controls that could fail. Placebo films, a label-shuffle chance baseline, a sequel check, word-count comparators, and dropping every sentence that still mentions bats, capes, clowns or caves.
  • Two models. Every result was rerun in Llama 3.1 8B; all three verdicts agree. The main difference: only Llama's neurons add anything to E2 beyond shared words.
  • Checked again afterwards. A robustness review rebuilt E2 from scratch, ran it 30,240 ways, and tested the Phase 1 claims against stronger chance baselines. Its scripts and outputs are in the repository.
  • Where we departed from the plan. Three pre-registered parts were not run at first and have been run now: the word-count controls for E1 and E2, and E2's beyond-words test. The E2 placebo check now uses the 9 valid placebo films, not the 11 in the plan. One caveat matters most. E2's frequency band (which memes count as optional) and hub adjustment were chosen after a simpler heredity test in Phase 1, on whole meme profiles, had failed, so they were not fixed before any data was seen. Rerunning the test under 30,240 combinations of reasonable settings is the check that the result does not depend on those choices. It limits that risk but cannot remove it.

What this can't tell you

  • Whether neurons see more than words. On these texts, word counts find the source comics as well as the neurons do. Qwen's neurons add nothing detectable beyond them, and Llama's add a little.
  • What makes Batman Batman. The canon score can't separate him from other superheroes without names. Word counts can, but mostly on crime-city vocabulary. Neither is a definition.
  • Anything about selection. E3 had about a 27% chance, roughly 1 in 4, of detecting a moderate true correlation of 0.3.
  • How stories were told at the time. Every text is a 2020s Wikipedia plot summary in one house style, so shared wording between summaries can carry part of the heredity signal.
  • How general this is. One franchise, 9 adaptations, 12 comics and two 8B models (8 billion parameters each) compressed to 4 bits.

What comes next

The aim is to say what Batman is, trace the shared history between Batmans, see when Batman mutates and which mutations catch on, and find Batman-like things across culture. New pre-registered experiments are being designed for each. None has run yet.

  • Neurons against the alternatives. A head-to-head of neurons, keywords, embedding vectors (number summaries of a whole text), topic models (which group words into themes) and an LLM, a large language model, reading the texts, scored on accuracy and cost.
  • Tests where shared wording can't carry the answer. Vocabulary drift over time, the same stories across languages, and stories with planted memes whose lineage is known in advance.
  • A fresh lineage. Shakespeare's plays and the works that descend from them, with the analysis fixed in advance.
  • Mutation and selection. Which changes to Batman catch on, compared with a neutral model of copying, in which memes are copied at random with no advantage.
  • The Batman family across culture. A scan for billionaire vigilante orphans far beyond the 13 relatives here.

Hypotheses worth testing

Some claims from marketing practice about how memes behave could be tested directly with a neuron-level measure.

  • The Lindy effect: the longer a meme has survived, the longer it will last. Test: does a meme's age in the lineage predict whether it appears in the next era?
  • Nostalgia cycles: faded memes return after about 30 years and feel new. Test: find memes that vanish and reappear, and check whether the gaps cluster near 30 years.
  • Memeplexes: some memes travel as a bundle. Test: which memes are gained and lost together along the tree?
  • Optimal distinctiveness: the hits sit just past the familiar. Test: plot success against a work's meme distance from its ancestors and look for an inverted U, with success peaking at a middle distance.
Phase 1

Measuring memes

Can a single neuron be a meme? We let Qwen3-8B read Wikipedia plot summaries of 32 Batman works, 13 relatives (older masked heroes, other superheroes, and classic tales and novels) and 69 ordinary films, and kept every neuron that fires far more for Batman than for ordinary films. A later robustness review tested these results against stronger controls. The core canon held up. The claims about agreement between models and about ideas rather than words came out narrower.

What does a story need to be Batman?

A meme is canon if it appears in nearly every Batman work. Of 14,843 memes, 56 appear in at least 29 of the 32, from the 1943 serial to The Batman (2022). We call a meme Batman's own if no more than 3 of the 13 relatives carry it; the rest he shares with other heroes. Most rare memes count as Batman's own simply because few relatives carry them.

The canon is a real property of Batman. When random sets of 32 ordinary films stand in for the franchise, scored the same way, not one neuron is present in even 23 of them. The number of memes is less fixed. The same procedure finds 24,732 in Llama and 5,641 in Qwen3-1.7B, a smaller model from the Qwen family, so the count belongs to the model and the thresholds as much as to Batman.

What to make of it

The strongest memes have near-identical twins: 70% of the top 33 have a twin above 0.7. Weak memes mostly don't. Across all 14,843, the typical twin is at 0.40, barely above neurons that aren't memes (0.36).

Some match is guaranteed, because each twin is the best of 917,504 Llama neurons; shuffling the sentences shows how much (0.14).

Most of the strongest memes detect names. By our reading, 24 of the top 33 are name or place detectors, and for these a single word predicts when the neuron fires almost exactly (correlation 0.94). Word-like neurons (ones a single word predicts) that are not memes have Llama twins about as good (0.86). With names removed, the same twin pairs fall to 0.14.

Concept memes, the ones the labeller marked as story ideas rather than names or words, do better. For the 27 labelled as concepts, twins reach 0.62, against 0.46 for neurons matched on word-likeness (p = 0.001). That edge holds on the original texts only; without names it is gone (0.34 against 0.38).

The meme grid (the Bat-genome, below) and the family trees (further down) need the same care. Random neurons are absent from most works, so random pairs matched for sparsity (how rarely they fire) agree on 89% to 92% of cells, mostly by both being empty. That is more than the memes' 87%. Kappa corrects for that: 0.73 for the memes against 0.35 to 0.57 for the random sets. The two models' trees agree at 0.98, but any two ways of measuring these texts agree closely. Random non-meme neurons give 0.90 to 0.93 between the models, and TF-IDF against LSA, two word-count methods, gives 0.92. With TF-IDF distances removed, the meme trees still agree at 0.92. Controlling for summary length and meme count leaves 0.90 to 0.98, depending on how the control is set up.

Same idea, different words

Counting words treats "revenge" and "vengeance" as unrelated and sees nothing in "make them pay". A neuron that tracks the idea should fire for all three. We found each model's revenge neuron from the 41 corpus sentences that use revenge words, then tested it on sentences it had never seen.

Numbers are each neuron's raw signal on the sentence. The two models' neurons work on different scales, so compare down a column, not across. Llama's neuron gives the harmless sentence its lowest score; Qwen's gives it the same score as the revenge sentence with no revenge word.

Llama's revenge neuron fires hardest on the sentence with no revenge word at all, and picked the revenge sentence over its harmless look-alike sentence in 10 of 12 new pairs. It also beat two harder sets of controls: sentences about harm with no revenge (AUC 0.87) and vows with no revenge (0.79). Qwen's treats "revenge" and "vengeance" alike, but without those words it was no better than a coin toss. Each model's unit (neuron) was picked from 50 candidates, and the two units fire on quite different corpus sentences (r = 0.39). So this is one good unit in one model.

We also reworded the top sentence of every named meme so it shares almost no words with the original (no content words for 28 of 31; three keep one common word: "let", "family" or "company"). A filled dot means the neuron fired more on the rewording than on any of ten everyday sentences.

Four memes survive in both models: a funeral, Two-Face's coin flip, the secret identity and the joke. In Qwen, every character meme except Harvey Dent fails once its name is gone; Llama's twins for the Riddler and Jason Todd survive.

The bar is lenient. A neuron also clears it on another meme's rewording 16% (Qwen) to 18% (Llama) of the time, so about 5 of 31 would survive by chance. Qwen's 11 beats that (p = 0.002); Llama's 9 does not clearly (p = 0.08). Only Qwen's funeral and coin neurons, and six of Llama's, fire more on their own rewording than on any other meme's. Even the funeral rewording keeps the funeral's vocabulary: mourners, an open grave, a coffin. So a few units carry an idea across rewording. Most do not.

The family tree

Works with similar meme fingerprints are joined first, using neighbour-joining, a standard way biologists build family trees from shared traits. It wasn't told dates, creators or eras; colours were added afterwards. The drawing shows the order of branching, not distances.

Every tree puts the three Arkham games on their own branch and Batman v Superman beside Superman. Every model pairs The Killing Joke with Joker (2019), which borrows the comic's failed-comedian origin. Qwen gathers a camp branch (Batman '66, Batman & Robin, The Lego Batman Movie, Batman Ninja); Llama gathers the grounded crime branch (Year One and The Long Halloween with the Nolan films and The Batman). Branches follow tone more than date.

The trees do not recover documented sources. Across the 9 adaptations, the real source ranks 5.4 (Qwen) and 5.3 (Llama) of 12 on average, where 1 would mean it is always the closest comic. That is close to the 5.8 expected by chance. TF-IDF puts it at 2.9, and random non-meme neurons at 3.2 to 4.3, so on this test the memes do worse than random neurons and worse than word counts. Phase 2's heredity test, which looks only at optional memes, does better.

What this can't tell you

  • That memes are the same in every model. The strong twins are mostly shared name and word detectors. Concept memes beat word-matched neurons, but only on texts with names.
  • That neurons track ideas in general. A few units carry an idea across rewording (the funeral, the coin flip, the secret identity, the joke, Llama's revenge unit). Most do not.
  • How many memes Batman has. The count depends on the model and thresholds: 14,843 in Qwen3-8B, 24,732 in Llama 3.1 8B and 5,641 in Qwen3-1.7B.
  • Exactly what each neuron is for. The labels passed a blind test (79% correct) but were never checked by steering, which means turning a neuron up or down and seeing whether the model's writing changes as the label predicts.
  • Who descends from whom, from the trees alone. The trees group works by tone, and they do not recover documented sources.

How it was set up

  • Texts. Wikipedia plot summaries, first 40 sentences each, 3,700 sentences: 32 Batman works (13 films and serials, 12 comics, 4 animated films, 3 games), 13 relatives, 69 ordinary films. Three works were dropped for having summaries under 8 sentences.
  • Models. Qwen3-8B (main), Llama 3.1 8B Instruct (independent check), Qwen3-1.7B (same family), all compressed to 4-bit numbers to run on a MacBook.
  • A neuron. One channel of a feed-forward layer, one of the model's stacked processing stages, with each end (positive, negative) counted separately: 884,736 in Qwen3-8B. An address like L13·8608+ is layer 13, neuron 8,608, positive end.
  • Present in a work. The neuron's average signal on the work's three strongest sentences beats its score on 68 of the 69 ordinary films. 18,485 neurons cleared that in at least 6 Batman works, merged into 14,843 memes. At a matched bar, Batman has 1.8 times as many such neurons as random sets of 32 ordinary films (Llama 2.4 times), and more than every one of 50 random sets. A simple chance calculation would expect about 230, but it treats the works as independent, and they are not.
  • Labels. Named by Claude from each neuron's top sentences and checked by a second, sceptical pass; not tested by steering. In a blind test, a judge given only a label picked the sentence its neuron fires on (one the labeller never saw) over a matched control 56 of 71 times (79%). A judge that only matches words between label and sentence scored 65%. For the 31 named memes it was 24 of 31 (77%), not clearly above the word judge (63%).
