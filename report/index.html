Phylomemetics · Batman 1943–2022

Neurons inside AI models make memes measurable, and the memes pass from Batman comics into the films that adapt them

Memetics stalled because nobody could say where one meme ends and the next begins. We defined a meme as whatever makes a single neuron inside an AI model fire, traced the memes that make up Batman across 80 years, checked them against a second model built by a different company, and ran three pre-registered experiments on identity, heredity and selection.

  • Phase 1 · MeasuringTwo models from different companies agree on the strong memes (correlation 0.82). Batman has 56 must-have memes; 24 are his own, 32 he shares with other heroes.
  • Phase 2 · ExperimentsHeredity holds: films carry their source comic's memes, in both models. Without names, Batman's canon is the superhero template, and Spider-Man, Daredevil and Superman carry it more strongly than most Batman stories. No sign that memes ride on hits.
  • Phase 3 · ImagesCLIP has visual Batman neurons (one fires on 28 of 29 posters and covers). DINOv2, trained without captions, has far fewer.

Phase 2

Three experiments: identity, heredity, selection

With a way to measure memes, can we test the three things a science of memes needs? Each experiment was written down, with the result that would count as failure, and attacked by three independent reviewers before any result was computed.

One of the three clearly works. Adaptations inherit their source comic's memes, even with every name removed, and both models agree (p = 0.004 and p = 0.0002). The identity test failed as stated and taught us something better: stripped of names, Batman's canon is the superhero template (a secret identity, an origin in loss, a costume, a mission by night), and Spider-Man, Daredevil and Superman carry it more strongly than most Batman stories do. The selection test found nothing, which with 15 early works is a weak test rather than evidence of no effect.

So Batman is two layers. The lower layer, the costumed hero with a secret identity and a wound, is a template he shares with other masked heroes. What is only Batman lives in the named memes: Gotham, Alfred, the Joker, Robin, the Batcave. E3 · Do memes ride on hits?

Question
Do the memes of popular early works (before 2000) turn up more in later works than the memes of obscure ones?
Prediction
Positive rank correlation between an early work's popularity (Wikipedia views per month) and how far its memes spread, with adaptations removed so fame from being adapted can't drive it.
Result
ρ = 0.25 (Qwen, p = 0.35) and 0.00 (Llama, p = 0.58). Using the ordinary films as a placebo outcome gives about the same ρ (0.29 and 0.26).

No link between an early work's popularity and how far its memes spread

Each dot is one of the 15 Batman works before 2000 (Qwen3-8B, name-free). Up means its memes reappear in later works more than their earlier frequency predicts, after accounting for the work's year, length and how typical it is.

With 15 works, only a strong effect could have shown up, so this is a weak null rather than evidence that popularity doesn't matter. Pageviews are also measured today, after decades of adaptation. A real test of selection needs a bigger lineage and success measured at release.

How we tried not to fool ourselves

  • Written down first. Each experiment's prediction and the result that would count as failure were fixed before any result was computed.
  • Attacked before running. Three AI reviewers were asked to break each design. They found real problems, and every fix below came from them.
  • Truly name-free texts. A rewriter first replaced names with role words, but called Batman "the vigilante" more than 300 times and Spider-Man "the hero". That leaks identity, so we used the rewrite only to find the names, then replaced each with a neutral code.
  • No marking our own homework. The films that set the presence bar are never scored; they are split in half 20 times.
  • Breaking circular loops. E3 drops every adaptation from the later works, because a comic's fame partly comes from being adapted. E2 counts only comics published no later than the film.
  • Controls that could fail. Placebo films, a label-shuffle null, a sequel positive control, and dropping every sentence that still mentions bats, capes, clowns or caves.
  • Two models. Every result was rerun in Llama 3.1 8B; all three verdicts agree.

What to test next, from Marketing Memetics

Taylor's book makes claims about how memes behave that a neuron-level measure can now test directly.

  • The Lindy effect (p. 95): the longer a meme has survived, the longer it will last. Test: does a meme's age in the lineage predict whether it appears in the next era?
  • Theft becomes nostalgia (pp. 22–23, 27): faded memes return after about 30 years and feel new. Test: find memes that vanish and reappear, and check whether the gaps cluster near 30 years.
  • Memeplexes (p. 190): some memes travel as a bundle. Test: which memes are gained and lost together along the tree?
  • Optimal distinctiveness (pp. 43, 101): the hits sit just past the familiar. Test: plot success against a work's meme distance from its ancestors and look for an inverted U.

What does a story need to be Batman?

A meme is canon if it appears in nearly every Batman work. Of 14,843 memes, 56 appear in at least 29 of the 32, from the 1943 serial to The Batman (2022). We call a meme Batman's own if no more than 3 of the 13 relatives carry it; the rest he shares with other heroes.

What to make of it

The strongest memes have near-identical twins: 70% of the top 33 have a twin above 0.7. Weak memes mostly don't. Across all 14,843, the typical twin is at 0.40, barely above neurons that aren't memes (0.36).

Some match is guaranteed, because each twin is the best of 917,504 Llama neurons; shuffling the sentences shows how much (0.14).

The tree agreement holds after controlling for how long each summary is (still 0.98).

Same idea, different words

Counting words treats "revenge" and "vengeance" as unrelated and sees nothing in "make them pay". A neuron that tracks the idea should fire for all three. We found each model's revenge neuron from the 41 corpus sentences that use revenge words, then tested it on sentences it had never seen.

Llama's revenge neuron fires hardest on the sentence with no revenge word at all, and picked the revenge sentence over its harmless twin in 10 of 12 new pairs. Qwen's treats "revenge" and "vengeance" alike, but without those words it was no better than a coin toss.

We also reworded the top sentence of every named meme so it shares almost no words with the original (no content words for 28 of 31; three keep one common word: "let", "family" or "company"). A filled dot means the neuron fired more on the rewording than on any of ten everyday sentences.

Four memes survive in both models: a funeral, Two-Face's coin flip, the secret identity and the joke. In Qwen, every character meme except Harvey Dent fails once its name is gone; Llama's twins for the Riddler and Jason Todd survive.

How it was set up

  • Texts. Wikipedia plot summaries, first 40 sentences each, 3,700 sentences: 32 Batman works (13 films and serials, 12 comics, 4 animated films, 3 games), 13 relatives, 69 ordinary films. Three works were dropped for having summaries under 8 sentences.
  • Models. Qwen3-8B (main), Llama 3.1 8B Instruct (independent check), Qwen3-1.7B (same family), all at 4-bit on a MacBook.
  • A neuron. One channel of a feed-forward layer, each end (positive, negative) counted separately: 884,736 in Qwen3-8B. An address like L13·8608+ is layer 13, neuron 8,608, positive end.
  • Present in a work. The work's three strongest sentences beat 68 of 69 ordinary films. 18,485 neurons cleared that in at least 6 Batman works (about 230 expected by chance), merged into 14,843 memes.
  • Labels. Named by Claude from each neuron's top sentences and checked by a second, sceptical pass; not tested by steering.

Phase 3 · exploratory

Memes in images

Does the same method find visual memes? We showed two vision models the lead image of each work's Wikipedia article: the poster, comic cover or game box art for 29 Batman works, 13 relatives and 69 ordinary films.

CLIP, trained on images paired with captions, has a visual Batman canon: one neuron fires on 28 of the 29 Batman images and on 1 ordinary-film poster. Ten neurons fire on at least half of them. DINOv2, trained on images alone, has more neurons but only 2 that fire on even 12 of the 29. Concept neurons seem to need language. The two vision models agree less than the two language models: CLIP's visual memes match DINOv2 neurons at a median correlation of 0.45, against 0.36 for random neurons and 0.13 by chance.

CLIP's visual Batman canon

The strongest CLIP neurons, named by reading the eight images each fires on most (labels checked by a second pass). Copyrighted posters and covers were analysed but are not shown.

Some of these are visual memes (the pointed cowl, the bat silhouette), some are text inside images (the BATMAN wordmark), and some are medium (drawn comic covers). A vision model's neurons mix all three, just as the language models' mixed story memes with Wikipedia's house style.

Testing the neurons on pictures we can show

We generated test images with no Batman in them and measured three visual-meme neurons on each. Numbers are percentiles against the neuron's responses to the 111 corpus images: 90 means it fired more strongly than on 90% of them.

The cowl neuron responds only moderately to gothic night scenes (around the 73rd percentile) and not at all to sunny ones; it wants the cowl itself. The hooded-figure and bat-wing neurons light up for a cloaked figure beside a gargoyle (96th and 88th percentile) and stay quiet for a beach or a kitchen. Visual memes look separable from generic darkness, but this is a handful of probes, not a test.

What this can't tell you

  • One image per work, and posters, covers and box art differ in kind, so some "memes" are really medium.
  • Three Batman covers couldn't be downloaded (Batman R.I.P., Earth One, Batman Ninja).
  • The labels come from looking at eight images per neuron; none were tested by steering.
